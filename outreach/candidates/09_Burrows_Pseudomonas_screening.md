# *Pseudomonas* screening — Lori Burrows (McMaster)

**Verified work.** According to PubMed, Burrows LL, *ACS Infectious Diseases* 4:1041–1047 (2018),
PMID 29771109, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1021/acsinfecdis.8b00112) — a *Pseudomonas* screening
pipeline with explicit attention to conditions under which standard assays mislead.

## The honest assessment: this is the weakest fit on the list

Their problems are substantially **spatial** — surface attachment, twitching motility, biofilm
architecture, pilus function. REM solves **well-mixed** master equations over small state spaces.
A spatially structured population is not a small well-mixed circuit, and the engine has no
validated capability there.

**This document exists to record that the offer is weak.** Sending a strong pitch here would be
dishonest and would cost credibility elsewhere on this list.

## The one point of genuine contact

Their 2018 paper is about **assay conditions changing what you conclude** — which is the same
concern that produced the two purity results in document 03 (Baliga/PerSort): a sorting efficiency
of 93%, entirely correct at a balanced mixture, gives **11.8% purity** on a 1% subpopulation, and
attenuates any measured difference **8.5×**.

That is a general statement about rare-subpopulation assays, verified against a 10⁷-cell simulation
to 0.08σ. It applies to any gate, sort, or enrichment on a minority population — including screens
where the interesting phenotype is rare.

## What is NOT claimed

- Nothing about their data. No inputs retrieved, nothing computed.
- No spatial capability of any kind is claimed.

## The question, as a question

*In any of your screens where the phenotype of interest is a small percentage of the population —
does the enrichment step's error rate get characterised at the prevalence it will actually be used
at, or at a balanced mixture? The two differ by more than intuition suggests and the arithmetic is
short.*
